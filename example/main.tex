\begin{abstract}
We train one small neural network on the two-moons problem with three optimisers:
stochastic gradient descent, SGD with momentum, and Adam, each over \nbval{n-seeds} random
seeds. On \nbval{n-test} held-out points their mean accuracies span \nbval{acc-spread}
percentage points (the best, \nbval{best}, reaches \nbval{best-acc}), and they differ even
more in speed: the fastest, \nbval{fastest}, brings the training loss below $0.1$ in
\nbval{fastest-epochs} epochs. Every figure, table and number in this paper is produced by
\texttt{notebooks/experiment.py} and updates when the notebook changes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Choosing an optimiser is one of the first decisions in any machine-learning experiment, and
one of the easiest to get wrong by habit. Plain stochastic gradient descent~\cite{robbins1951}
is simple and well understood; momentum~\cite{polyak1964} and Adam~\cite{kingma2015} promise
faster progress at the cost of extra state and extra hyperparameters. This note compares the
three on a problem small enough to rerun in under a second, so the comparison can be checked
rather than taken on trust.

Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the model and the three update rules,
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports what happened, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} says what
we take from it.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
On this problem, with a fixed budget of \nbval{epochs} epochs, the choice of optimiser
decided how much of the training got done within it. That is a statement about one small
network and one dataset; the notebook
makes it cheap to find out how far it carries, and Appendix~\ref{app:reproducibility}
records exactly how these numbers were produced.
